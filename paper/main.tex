\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Carbon capture, utilisation and storage (CCUS) is expected to play a substantial role in the
decarbonisation of industry and power generation. Where a capture site is not connected directly
to a pipeline for gaseous or dense-phase export, the captured CO$_2$ is liquefied and held in
intermediate storage before onward transport by truck, train or ship. This intermediate storage
is typically operated at low to medium pressure, in the range of approximately 7 to 20\,bara,
where liquid CO$_2$ can be kept at a moderately low temperature. Intermediate storage is also
used at the receiving end of the chain, ahead of transfer to injection for geological storage,
as in the Norwegian Longship / Northern Lights project at {\O}ygarden \citep{Helgesen2021} and
the recently commissioned Greensand project in Denmark \citep{Starodubtseva2024}. The role of
liquefied CO$_2$ storage and transport in the value chain, and its cost and environmental
trade-offs, have been examined for both trucking and industrial-cluster logistics
\citep{Ashkavand2025, Hennequin2026}.

A growing number of European capture projects rely on such intermediate storage of liquefied
CO$_2$. Examples include the Brevik CCS facility at the Heidelberg Materials cement plant in
Norway \citep{Burns2024}, the commercial capture plant at AVR in Duiven and the plant at Twence
in Hengelo, both in the Netherlands \citep{Ros2022}, the {\O}rsted carbon capture projects at
the Aved{\o}re and Asn{\ae}s power stations in Denmark \citep{HoeegJohansen2025}, and the capture
plant at the Yara fertiliser complex in Sluiskil, the Netherlands. In each case liquid CO$_2$ is
buffered on site before it is loaded for transport.

Reliable operation and design of this part of the value chain requires an understanding of the
behaviour of CO$_2$ at intermediate storage and transport conditions, and in particular of its
phase behaviour. Because the atmospheric sublimation pressure of CO$_2$ lies below the triple
point ($P_\text{tp} = 5.18$\,bar, $T_\text{tp} = -56.6\,^\circ$C), any depressurisation that
approaches or crosses the triple point moves the fluid towards the vapour and solid region. An
unintentional depressurisation of a storage vessel therefore raises two distinct concerns.
Operationally, expansion below the triple point forms solid CO$_2$ (dry ice), which sublimes
slowly and can obstruct valves, lines and instrumentation. Mechanically, the expansion cools the
fluid and the vessel wall well below the normal storage temperature, so that the wall may fall
below its minimum design temperature and risk brittle behaviour.

When such a depressurisation results from an accidental event, for example a leak or the rupture
of associated piping or a nozzle, the release must be characterised for safety assessment. The
thermo-mechanical response of the vessel, the pressure-decay trajectory, and above all the
release rate and the conditions of the released stream are needed as input to consequence
modelling. These quantities feed dispersion studies and quantitative risk assessment (QRA), and
they also provide the source-term boundary conditions for more rigorous computational
fluid-dynamics (CFD) analysis of the near-field jet and dispersion.

Much of the quantitative understanding of accidental CO$_2$ release has been developed in the
context of pipeline transport rather than vessel storage. The dense-phase transmission pipeline,
for which a full-bore rupture or a puncture produces a rapid decompression on a timescale of
seconds, has been the subject of large joint-industry programmes and of a substantial modelling
literature. Depressurisation experiments in instrumented pipes have been reported for pure CO$_2$
and for CO$_2$ mixtures, together with two-phase flow models based on a homogeneous description of
the fluid \citep{Munkejord2015, Munkejord2021}. Discharge through the break has been treated both
with the homogeneous equilibrium assumption and, where the departure from equilibrium during the
rapid expansion is significant, with a homogeneous relaxation model \citep{Brown2013, Brown2014}.
Larger-scale orifice release data and their review have been produced within the CO2PIPETRANS
programme \citep{Witlox2015}, and integrated frameworks coupling the pipeline source term to
near-field and far-field dispersion have been assembled for hazard assessment \citep{Woolley2014}.
This body of work has established the essential physics of CO$_2$ decompression, but it addresses
a geometry, a pressure level and a timescale that differ from those of an intermediate-storage
vessel.

A common feature of these studies is the treatment of the flow through the release path itself,
which is typically choked. The mass flux at the throat depends strongly on whether the expanding
fluid is assumed to remain in phase equilibrium or to depart from it. The homogeneous equilibrium
model assumes instantaneous phase change and mechanical and thermal equilibrium between the
phases, whereas non-equilibrium and relaxation formulations retain a degree of liquid superheat
or metastability during the rapid expansion, which tends to raise the predicted mass flow
relative to the equilibrium value \citep{Lopes2018, Brown2013}. Experimental characterisation of
choked CO$_2$ flow through orifices and nozzles, including the transition through the triple
point, has been reported and used to assess these models \citep{Pursell2012, Hammer2022}.
Downstream of the throat the stream expands to atmospheric pressure along an approximately
isenthalpic path, cooling below the triple point so that a fraction of the flow forms solid
CO$_2$. The partition between vapour and solid, the temperature of the released stream and the
resulting jet structure are the source-term quantities required by dispersion modelling, and they
have been examined experimentally and through jet-release and dispersion models
\citep{Toesse2013, Wareing2014, Dixon2012}; the sublimation of a deposited dry-ice bank as a
continuing source has also been treated \citep{Mazzoldi2008}. Some consequence tools carry this
decomposition explicitly, separating the in-vessel state, the discharge through the restriction
and the post-expansion state that is passed to the dispersion calculation \citep{Witlox2012}.

By comparison with the pipeline literature, dedicated experiments on the blowdown of CO$_2$
storage vessels are more recent and fewer. Accidental leakage of liquid CO$_2$ under shipping
conditions has been studied on a bench-scale vessel with an orifice, with the vapour and solid
fractions of the discharge quantified \citep{AlBaroudi2021}, and the discharge from a marine
loading-arm emergency release coupler has been investigated at real scale \citep{AlBaroudi2022}.
A visualised depressurisation matrix spanning the gas, liquid and supercritical regions has
recently been reported for a small vessel \citep{Chen2026}. At the high-pressure, dense-phase end,
the SINTEF and NTNU experiments on a densely instrumented vertical vessel provide pressure,
temperature and mass-flow data together with dedicated nozzle-flow measurements
\citep{Hoydalsvik2026, Hammer2022}. For the low- and medium-pressure two-phase conditions that are
most representative of intermediate storage, the most elaborate dataset is that of the CARDICE
project \citep{Vaillant2021, Jamois2023}: a 2\,m$^3$ sphere instrumented with distributed fluid
and wall thermocouples and continuous weighing, configurable for release from either the vapour
space or the liquid space, and spanning approximately 10 to 20\,bara and $-20$ to
$-50\,^\circ$C.

The CARDICE experiments have been simulated with three separate tools, and the comparisons are
instructive. The commercial fire- and blowdown-response tool VessFire \citep{Vessfire} reproduced
the vapour-space release and the associated solid CO$_2$ formation well \citep{Vaillant2021}. A
process-simulator blowdown utility was applied to the low- and medium-pressure tests
\citep{Drescher2022}; although its underlying property package is reported to include a
description of solid CO$_2$, this capability was not exercised in the reported simulations, and
the authors note the absence of a validated method for the liquid-orifice flow when the fluid
departs from equilibrium. The multiphase flow simulator LedaFlow \citep{Teberikler2022}
reproduced the liquid-space release adequately, but was less successful for the vapour-space
release where dry ice forms. Each of these tools captures part of the behaviour, but all are
proprietary and none is distributed as open source, so the underlying models cannot be inspected,
modified or reused.

The most detailed model of this kind is the one developed alongside the SINTEF and NTNU experiments
\citep{Hoydalsvik2026}. Its modelling basis is disclosed: it couples a multi-phase flash of the
bulk fluid, a nozzle-flow discharge model and a detailed two-dimensional (conjugate) heat-transfer
description of the vessel wall, and it reproduces the measured dense-phase depressurisation well.
The full model is not, however, in the public domain, and it assumes full thermodynamic equilibrium
between the gas and liquid phases, so that a single bulk fluid temperature is carried and thermal
stratification between the phases is not represented. Taken together with the CARDICE simulations
above, there is at present no open and freely available tool that has been validated for CO$_2$
vessel blowdown across both vapour- and liquid-space releases and across the low, medium and
high-pressure range.

HydDown \citep{Andreasen2021, Andreasen2026} is an open-source, Python-based tool for the
transient simulation of pressure-vessel filling and depressurisation, including real-fluid
thermodynamics through CoolProp \citep{Bell2014}, several mass-flow models, and detailed internal
and wall heat transfer. It provides a suitable open-source foundation for addressing the gap
identified above, but its thermodynamic backend has two limitations at sub-triple conditions.
First, CoolProp includes no solid CO$_2$ model, so dry-ice formation cannot be represented.
Second, its equilibrium flash resolves only vapour and liquid states and therefore cannot follow
a fluid that expands below the triple point into the vapour and solid region. The underlying
reference equation of state \citep{Span1996} can nonetheless be evaluated for the single-phase gas
below the triple point, a feature that the present work exploits. The purpose of the present paper
is to extend HydDown with the models and thermodynamic treatment needed to simulate the
depressurisation of a CO$_2$ storage vessel at intermediate storage and transport conditions,
including the formation of dry ice both inside the vessel and downstream of the release, and to
validate the extension against experiment. The specific objectives are to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item extend the thermodynamic treatment below the triple point so that the vapour and solid
        regions become accessible, by combining imposed-phase evaluation of the reference equation
        of state for the gas with a precomputed solid-CO$_2$ property table;
  \item formulate the discharge, in-vessel dry-ice and heat-transfer models for both vapour-space
        and liquid-space releases, accounting both for the dry ice retained inside the vessel and
        for the solid fraction of the released stream downstream of the orifice, leak or rupture;
        and
  \item validate the extended model against two independent experimental campaigns spanning the
        low-, medium- and high-pressure range.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

The model developed here extends HydDown \citep{Andreasen2021, Andreasen2026}, an open-source
Python tool for the transient simulation of pressure-vessel filling and depressurisation. HydDown
integrates the vessel mass and energy balances with an explicit time-marching scheme, evaluates
real-fluid properties through CoolProp \citep{Bell2014}, and provides several discharge models
together with internal and external heat-transfer options. For a two-phase inventory it follows a
partial-phase-equilibrium description in which the vapour and the liquid are carried as two
separate zones, each with its own temperature and coupled by a finite interphase heat transfer,
rather than as a single well-mixed fluid at one temperature; this two-zone, non-equilibrium
description (NEM) is due to \citet{SperanzaTerenzi2005} and is implemented in HydDown as reported
by \citet{AndreasenStegelmann2026}. It is motivated by the experimental observation that during a
blowdown the two phases follow distinct temperature paths, and that the wall in contact with the
boiling liquid cools more strongly than the wall in contact with the vapour
\citep{SperanzaTerenzi2005}. It contrasts with a full-equilibrium treatment, in which a single
bulk temperature is carried and thermal stratification between the phases is not represented
(Section~\ref{sec:introduction}).

The present work retains this two-zone framework and extends it below the triple point so that
solid CO$_2$ (dry ice) can be represented, both as a bank retained inside the vessel and as the
solid fraction of the released stream. The extension is limited to pure CO$_2$ and to two release
geometries, a vapour-space (gas) release and a liquid-space (liquid) release, and it admits an
initial state that is either a dense-phase or subcooled liquid or a saturated liquid and vapour
mixture. The remainder of this section sets out the phase logic
(Section~\ref{sec:model-logic}), the thermodynamic treatment and the solid-CO$_2$ lever
(Section~\ref{sec:thermo}), the vessel mass and energy balances (Section~\ref{sec:balance}), the
in-vessel dry-ice model (Section~\ref{sec:dryice}), the discharge model
(Section~\ref{sec:discharge}), the heat- and mass-transfer correlations (Section~\ref{sec:heat}),
and the downstream dry-ice split (Section~\ref{sec:atmdryice}).

\subsection{Modelling approach and phase logic}
\label{sec:model-logic}

A CO$_2$ blowdown passes through a sequence of thermodynamic regimes, and the discharge model, the
in-vessel treatment, the heat-transfer law and the phase lever each change as the fluid crosses a
regime boundary. The overall logic is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:phasemap}. Both admissible initial
states, a dense-phase or subcooled liquid (as in the SINTEF tests) and a saturated liquid and
vapour mixture (as in the CARDICE tests), enter the same decision tree. The first branch is the
release location: a vapour-space release follows the left spine (regimes P1 to P3) and a
liquid-space release the right spine (regimes L1 to L3).

For a vapour-space release the vessel first depressurises with the liquid and vapour close to
equilibrium (P1). When the pressure reaches the triple point the liquid begins to freeze and the
pressure is held on a plateau while liquid converts to solid at fixed temperature (P2). Once the
liquid is exhausted the vessel rides the sublimation line down to the back pressure while the
retained dry-ice bank slowly sublimes (P3). For a liquid-space release the liquid is drawn off and
boils as the pressure falls (L1), the two-phase inventory drains towards a heel where the draw
changes over to the vapour space (L2), and the residual vapour then blows down (L3). In this case
the liquid leaves the vessel before the triple point is reached, so essentially no dry ice is
retained inside the vessel; the solid instead forms downstream of the orifice. In every regime the
released stream is separately flashed to atmospheric pressure to give the downstream vapour and
solid split (Section~\ref{sec:atmdryice}). The model in force in each regime is summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:phasemodels}.

\subsection{Thermodynamics and the solid-CO$_2$ lever}
\label{sec:thermo}

Above the triple point the fluid properties are those of the CoolProp reference equation of state
for CO$_2$ \citep{Span1996, Bell2014}, and the two zones are the equilibrium liquid and vapour.
Below the triple point two additions are required, because the CoolProp backend has no solid phase
and its equilibrium flash resolves only vapour and liquid (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}).

First, the vapour properties below the triple point are obtained by imposing the gas phase on the
CoolProp state, which makes the reference equation of state extrapolate smoothly into the
metastable gas region past the triple-point boundary. Every sub-triple vapour property (density,
internal energy, enthalpy, entropy and the discharge flux integrand) is taken from this
imposed-gas state. The extrapolation was checked against an independent GERG-2008 reference and
agrees to within about 0.01\,K in temperature and to four decimals in phase fraction down to about
1\,bar. Second, the solid branch, which CoolProp cannot supply, is provided by a small property
table computed once and tabulated along the sublimation line $P_\text{sub}(T)$; the tabulated
quantities are listed in Table~\ref{tab:solidtable}. The solid enthalpy and entropy are
re-referenced into the CoolProp basis through the physical latent heat of sublimation at each
temperature, so that a solid property and a CoolProp vapour property may be combined on one
consistent basis. The table is generated offline and stored with the code; at runtime the model
uses only CoolProp and the tabulated solid values.

Below the triple point pure CO$_2$ is a one-component, two-phase (vapour and solid) system, so by
the Gibbs phase rule it has a single degree of freedom: fixing the pressure fixes the temperature
$T_\text{sub}(P)$ and both pure-phase states. Every flash below the triple point therefore reduces
to lever-rule arithmetic on the sublimation line, and no solid-aware equation-of-state solver is
required. The pure-phase reference states are anchored at a self-consistent triple point,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:triple}
T_\text{tp} = 216.59~\text{K}, \qquad P_\text{tp} = 5.18~\text{bar},
\end{equation}
at which the specific volumes, enthalpies and internal energies of solid, liquid and vapour
($v_{s},v_{l},v_{g}$ and $u_{s},u_{l},u_{g}$) are stored as the vertices of the phase lever: the
solid vertex from the table, and the liquid and vapour vertices from CoolProp saturation at
$P_\text{tp}$. The latent heat of sublimation follows as $L_\text{sub} = h_g - h_s$.

\subsection{Vessel mass and energy balances}
\label{sec:balance}

The total inventory decreases at the discharge rate,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:massbal}
\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\dot m,
\end{equation}
with $\dot m$ from the discharge model (Section~\ref{sec:discharge}). In the two-zone description
the vapour and liquid masses ($m_g$, $m_l$) additionally exchange through interphase evaporation
and condensation at rates $\dot m_\text{ev}$ and $\dot m_\text{cd}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zonemass}
\frac{\mathrm{d}m_g}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\dot m_g + \dot m_\text{ev} - \dot m_\text{cd},
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}m_l}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\dot m_l - \dot m_\text{ev} + \dot m_\text{cd},
\end{equation}
where $\dot m_g$ and $\dot m_l$ are the mass rates leaving the vapour and liquid zones. The outflow
is attributed to the phase the mass actually leaves from, so exactly one of $\dot m_g$, $\dot m_l$
is non-zero: a vapour-space release draws vapour and a liquid-space release draws liquid, with
$\dot m_g + \dot m_l = \dot m$. Each zone integrates its own internal energy,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:zoneenergy}
\frac{\mathrm{d}U_g}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \dot Q_{wg} - \dot Q_{gl}
      - \dot m_g\,h_g^\text{out} + \dot m_\text{ev}\,h_\text{ev} - \dot m_\text{cd}\,h_\text{cd}, \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}U_l}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \dot Q_{wl} + \dot Q_{gl}
      - \dot m_l\,h_l^\text{out} - \dot m_\text{ev}\,h_\text{ev} + \dot m_\text{cd}\,h_\text{cd},
\nonumber
\end{align}
where $\dot Q_{wg}$ and $\dot Q_{wl}$ are the wall-to-vapour and wall-to-liquid heat rates
(Section~\ref{sec:heat}), $\dot Q_{gl} = h_{gl}\,A_i\,(T_g - T_l)$ is the interphase heat rate
across the interface of area $A_i$, and $h_g^\text{out}$, $h_l^\text{out}$, $h_\text{ev}$,
$h_\text{cd}$ are the specific enthalpies of the discharged and phase-transferred mass. The zone
temperatures $T_g$, $T_l$ and the shared pressure follow from the zone states $(m_g, U_g)$ and
$(m_l, U_l)$ under the constraint that the two zones fill the vessel volume. Because both wall
terms are retained, the wall exchanges with each zone independently, which is what allows the warm
vapour and the cold boiling liquid to follow separate temperature paths.

\subsection{In-vessel dry ice: triple-point plateau and sublimation descent}
\label{sec:dryice}

A vapour-space release keeps the liquid in the vessel, so the liquid cools to the triple point,
freezes, and a dry-ice bank is retained, after which the vessel blows down on the sublimation line.
This behaviour is captured by two below-triple regimes.

On the \emph{plateau} the triple point is invariant, so the temperature and pressure are pinned and
the pure-phase specific volumes and internal energies are constants. The phase masses then follow
from conservation of total mass, volume and internal energy,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:plateau}
\begin{aligned}
m_g + m_l + m_s &= M, \\
m_g v_g + m_l v_l + m_s v_s &= V, \\
m_g u_g + m_l u_l + m_s u_s &= U,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $V$ the vessel volume. As the leak removes mass and heat enters, $M$ and $U$ change and the
lever redistributes the phases, converting liquid to solid at fixed $T$ and $P$; this produces the
pressure plateau observed in vapour-space blowdowns. The vapour is carried as a separate warm zone
that keeps its own temperature, heated by the wall and only weakly coupled to the cold liquid and
solid, so that the model reproduces the measured vapour superheat and the freezing lever
simultaneously. While liquid remains, the wall in the condensate region is boiling-cooled by the
liquid (Section~\ref{sec:heat}) and this heat enters the liquid and solid zone, boiling some liquid
off rather than allowing all of it to freeze.

On the \emph{descent}, once the liquid is exhausted, the state rides the sublimation line. A warm
vapour zone leaks and depressurises while the dry-ice bank sublimes only enough to cool itself as
$T_\text{sub}(P)$ falls and to absorb the vapour-to-solid interphase heat,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:descent}
\begin{aligned}
\dot m_\text{sub} &= -\,\frac{m_s\,c_{p,s}}{L_\text{sub}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}T_\text{sub}}{\mathrm{d}t}
                  + \frac{\dot Q_{gs}}{L_\text{sub}}, \\
\dot Q_{gs} &= h_{gs}\,A_{gs}\,(T_g - T_s).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The interphase heat acts over the gas-solid interface area $A_{gs}$, the horizontal cross-section
at the top of the settled dry-ice bed, derived from the solid mass and the vessel geometry, and
not the much larger vapour-wall contact area; using the bed-top cross-section prevents an
over-large coupling from dragging the well-mixed vapour down to $T_\text{sub}$. The interphase
coefficient $h_{gs}$ is the single parameter that trades vapour superheat against retained solid:
with it small the dry-ice bank is near-adiabatic and largely retained. The dry-ice region wall
tracks $T_\text{sub}(P)$ (Section~\ref{sec:heat}).

\subsection{Discharge model}
\label{sec:discharge}

The release rate is computed with a homogeneous-equilibrium model (HEM) with a solid-aware throat,
supplemented by an optional non-equilibrium boost for a flashing liquid. It is evaluated on the
CoolProp and solid-table basis at all pressures, so the mass rate is continuous across the triple
point: only the vessel state hands over there, not the discharge model. The flow is assumed
homogeneous and isentropic from the stagnation state to the throat. Along the isentrope $s = s_0$
the mass flux at throat pressure $P$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hem}
G(P) = \rho(P)\,\sqrt{2\,[\,h_0 - h(P)\,]}, \qquad s(P) = s_0,
\end{equation}
and the discharge maximises $G$ over the throat pressure between the back pressure and the
stagnation pressure,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdot}
\dot m = C_d\,A\,G_\text{max}, \qquad G_\text{max} = \max_{P_\text{back}\,\le\, P\,\le\, P_0} G(P),
\end{equation}
with $A$ the orifice area, $C_d$ the discharge coefficient, and $h_0$, $s_0$, $\rho_0$ the
stagnation enthalpy, entropy and density. The stagnation state is a saturated (or dense) liquid for
a liquid release, a saturated vapour for a vapour release, and an explicit vapour state on the
sublimation line below the triple point. Above the triple point the throat is a native CoolProp
state; below it the throat is a vapour and dry-ice mixture resolved by the sublimation-line lever,
so its density and enthalpy carry the solid fraction, and a stream that expands below the triple
point correctly forms solid at the orifice. At a representative vapour condition the flux of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hem} agrees with a textbook choked ideal-gas orifice to within a few percent, which
confirms that the flux itself is correct and that the modelling choices lie in the feed phase and
the discharge coefficient.

A saturated liquid discharged through a short, sharp orifice has too little residence time to flash
to equilibrium, so the metastable liquid persists past its saturation pressure and the flux lies
above the equilibrium value, towards the frozen all-liquid limit \citep{Fauske1985, Dyer2007}. The
frozen incompressible (Bernoulli) flux
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:frozen}
G_\text{frozen} = \sqrt{2\,\rho_L\,(P_0 - P_\text{back})}
\end{equation}
is the upper bound and the equilibrium HEM flux the lower bound; the two are combined with a single
non-equilibrium factor $N \in [0,1]$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hne}
G_\text{liq} = \sqrt{(1-N)\,G_\text{HEM}^2 + N\,G_\text{frozen}^2},
\end{equation}
so that $N = 0$ recovers equilibrium HEM and $N = 1$ the frozen liquid. The blend is applied only
to a liquid stagnation; a single-phase vapour discharge has nothing to flash and is unchanged. For
saturated CO$_2$ at 10 to 20\,bar the frozen-to-HEM flux ratio is large, of order four, because the
HEM flux chokes on the low two-phase sound speed while the dense liquid drives a much higher
Bernoulli flux, so a modest $N$ produces a substantial boost. Reconciling $N$ against the measured
steady liquid-drain rate at a fixed liquid discharge coefficient shows that, across the saturated
regime, $N$ scales approximately linearly with the distance above the triple point,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nofp}
N \approx 0.013\,(P_0 - P_\text{tp}) \quad [\,P \text{ in bar}\,],
\end{equation}
vanishing at the triple point, and this scaling is used to fade each case's calibrated $N$ as the
vessel blows down.

The discharge coefficient is split by phase. The vapour discharge is single-phase HEM and takes a
sharp-edged value $C_{d,\text{gas}} = 0.84$; the coefficient fitted to the gas releases is
essentially constant along each pressure decline, consistent with the HEM description. The liquid
discharge takes the two-phase, flashing sharp-orifice value $C_{d,\text{liq}} = 0.62$
\citep{Darby2004} together with the non-equilibrium factor $N$, so a liquid release uses
$C_{d,\text{liq}}$ for the liquid and $C_{d,\text{gas}}$ for its vapour tail. The non-equilibrium
boost is a short-residence phenomenon and is kept independent of hole diameter, calibrated per case
and faded only with pressure. For a full-bore or large-diameter rupture the long flow path lets the
liquid flash to equilibrium, so the appropriate choice is $C_d = 1$ with $N = 0$; the small-orifice
boost should not be added on top of $C_d = 1$, because the discharge coefficient and the
non-equilibrium factor represent different physics and anti-correlate with hole size. For the
high-pressure dense regime, plain HEM with a fixed discharge coefficient reproduces the measured
flux about as well as a homogeneous non-equilibrium model \citep{Lopes2018, Hammer2022}, so the
additional non-equilibrium machinery is not required there.

\subsection{Heat and mass transfer}
\label{sec:heat}

The wall is resolved into two nodes, an unwetted (vapour-contact) node at temperature $T_w$ and a
wetted (liquid- or solid-contact) node at $T_w^{w}$, with the wetted and unwetted areas taken from
the vessel geometry at the current liquid level. Each node exchanges with the ambient through an
external coefficient $h_\text{out}$, taken as the measured value of approximately
0.4\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$ for the insulated CARDICE sphere (Section~\ref{sec:experimental}).

Above the triple point the inner vapour- and liquid-side coefficients follow the Geankoplis
natural-convection correlation \citep{Geankoplis1993} in terms of the Rayleigh number
$\mathrm{Ra} = \mathrm{Gr}\,\mathrm{Pr}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:geankoplis}
\mathrm{Nu} =
\begin{cases}
0.13\,\mathrm{Ra}^{1/3} & \mathrm{Ra} \ge 10^9,\\[2pt]
0.59\,\mathrm{Ra}^{1/4} & 10^4 < \mathrm{Ra} < 10^9,\\[2pt]
1.36\,\mathrm{Ra}^{1/5} & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad h = \frac{\mathrm{Nu}\,k}{L},
\end{equation}
with $L$ the vessel height (vertical) or diameter (horizontal) and the fluid properties from
CoolProp. Below the triple point the vapour-contact wall uses the Churchill--Chu free-convection
correlation for a vertical surface \citep{ChurchillChu1975}, the same law adopted in the SINTEF
reference model \citep{Hoydalsvik2026},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:churchill}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Nu} &= \left(0.825 +
\frac{0.387\,\mathrm{Ra}_{L_v}^{1/6}}{[\,1 + (0.492/\mathrm{Pr})^{9/16}\,]^{8/27}}\right)^2, \\
h_{gw} &= \frac{\mathrm{Nu}\,k}{L_v},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
evaluated with the CO$_2$ vapour properties at the film temperature from the imposed-gas state and
with the characteristic length equal to the vapour-column height $L_v$ above the settled
condensate, which shrinks as solid and liquid accumulate. The single-equation form is smooth across
the laminar-turbulent transition and tends to a finite conduction floor as the vapour-to-wall
temperature difference vanishes.

While liquid is present the wetted wall is cooled by nucleate pool boiling of the liquid, and this
heat flows into the liquid and solid zone as an additional vaporisation term. The boiling
coefficient uses, by default, the Cooper reduced-pressure correlation \citep{Cooper1984},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cooper}
h_\text{boil} = 55\,P_r^{\,0.12 - 0.2\log_{10} R_p}\,(-\log_{10} P_r)^{-0.55}\,M^{-0.5}\,q^{0.67},
\end{equation}
in terms of the reduced pressure $P_r = P/P_c$, the surface roughness $R_p$, the molar mass $M$ and
the wall heat flux $q$. Cooper is preferred because it requires no surface tension, saturated
densities or latent heat, quantities that are ill-defined for CO$_2$ at and below the triple point;
the Rohsenow correlation \citep{Rohsenow1952}, which needs all of them, is available as an
alternative. Both are capped at 3000\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$, above which the wall is
conduction-limited and its temperature is insensitive to the exact coefficient. Once the liquid is
exhausted the wetted wall is buried against the cold dry-ice bed and is held on the sublimation
line,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:icewall}
T_w^{w} = T_\text{sub}(P) \qquad \text{(while dry ice is present)},
\end{equation}
so that no wall-to-solid heat is applied and the retained dry-ice mass is unaffected by the wall.
When a phase is depleted its wall node clamps to the surviving phase's node to avoid a stranded
temperature.

The gas-liquid interphase heat rate $\dot Q_{gl}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:zoneenergy} acts across the
horizontal interface, whose area $A_i$ is the vessel cross-section at the current liquid level,
evaluated for the vessel geometry and orientation. The interphase coefficient $h_{gl}$ is taken
either as a fixed value or from a horizontal-interface free-convection correlation. In the
correlation option the coefficient is evaluated on both sides of the interface and combined as two
boundary-layer resistances in series,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hgl}
\frac{1}{h_{gl}} = \frac{1}{h_{gl}^{\,g}} + \frac{1}{h_{gl}^{\,l}},
\end{equation}
where each side is obtained from a horizontal-surface natural-convection law evaluated with the
properties of the respective phase, in a form selected by whether the stratification is stable
(warm vapour above cold liquid) or unstable. This two-sided coefficient is used for all cases, both
the gas and the liquid releases. Below the triple point the vapour-to-liquid coefficient on the
plateau is instead kept near zero, so that the warm vapour stays decoupled from the freezing
condensate, and
the vapour-to-solid coefficient in the descent acts over the bed-top interface area of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:descent} rather than over the interface at the liquid level.

For a vapour-space release the single, well-mixed vapour node runs colder than the measured warm
top of the vessel, because the real vapour is thermally stratified, warm at the top and cold near
the interface, which a zero-dimensional node cannot represent. This is a limitation of the
zero-dimensional description rather than of the heat-transfer coefficients, and the same gap is
seen in other tools with a single-node vapour zone \citep{Vaillant2021}; it is revisited with the
results (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Downstream (atmospheric) dry ice}
\label{sec:atmdryice}

The dry ice produced downstream of the orifice is estimated from the released stream. A free jet
does no external work, so the stream conserves its stagnation enthalpy as it expands to atmospheric
pressure, and below the triple point the vapour and solid split is again a single-degree-of-freedom
lever. It is computed as an isenthalpic lever between the pure-vapour and pure-solid enthalpies at
the back pressure,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:atmlever}
\beta_\text{gas} = \frac{h_0 - h_\text{solid}^\text{atm}}{h_\text{gas}^\text{atm} - h_\text{solid}^\text{atm}},
\qquad
x_\text{solid} = 1 - \operatorname{clip}(\beta_\text{gas}, 0, 1),
\end{equation}
where $h_0$ is the stream stagnation enthalpy and $h_\text{gas}^\text{atm}$,
$h_\text{solid}^\text{atm}$ are the pure-vapour and pure-solid enthalpies at the frost point
($T_\text{frost} \approx 194$\,K, about $-79\,^\circ$C at 1\,atm). If $\beta_\text{gas} \ge 1$ the
stream is superheated and no dry ice forms; otherwise it is a vapour and dry-ice mixture at the
frost point. For a liquid release the discharged stream is typically about 50\,\% dry ice by mass
at atmospheric pressure, so the dry ice a liquid release produces appears here, downstream of the
orifice, rather than inside the vessel.

\section{Experimental basis}
\label{sec:experimental}

The model is validated against two independent experimental campaigns which together span the
pressure range relevant for intermediate storage and transport of CO$_2$: the low- and
medium-pressure CARDICE experiments performed by Ineris in a 2\,m$^3$ sphere, and the
high-pressure, dense-phase experiments performed by SINTEF and NTNU in a small, densely
instrumented cylinder. The two campaigns are complementary. In the former the vessel reaches
the triple point and a large dry-ice bank is retained inside the vessel, whereas in the latter
the fluid starts in the dense-liquid region and flashes vigorously through the discharge nozzle.
Both datasets are openly available.

\subsection{CARDICE (Ineris 2\,m$^3$ sphere)}
\label{sec:exp-cardice}

The CARDICE (CARbon DIoxide iCE) Joint Industry Project comprises pilot-scale CO$_2$ blowdown
experiments carried out by Ineris in a 2\,m$^3$ steel sphere. The test matrix and a comparison
with the commercial tool Vessfire are reported by \citet{Vaillant2021}, while the rig,
instrumentation and calibration are documented in detail by \citet{Jamois2023}. The 1\,Hz
dataset is openly available \citep{CardiceData2024}.

The vessel is a sphere formed from two welded hemispheres, with an internal volume of
2.030\,m$^3$, an inner diameter of 1556\,mm and a mean wall thickness of 54\,mm. It is
fabricated from P355GH steel and rated for 200\,bara at $-60\,^\circ$C, with a total mass of
approximately 4.8\,t. The sphere is insulated with 100\,mm of rubber foam. A cooling
calorimetry test reported by \citet{Jamois2023} gives an overall external heat-transfer
conductance of 4.3\,W\,K$^{-1}$, equivalent to approximately 0.4\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$ over the
11\,m$^2$ outer surface. For the several-hour blowdowns this heat ingress is small relative to
the latent loads, so the process is effectively near-adiabatic.

CO$_2$ is released through a release pipe of 40\,mm inner diameter and approximately 2\,m
length, terminated by a calibrated orifice connected either to the middle of the sphere for a
vapour-space release or to the bottom for a liquid-space release. The measured quantities
comprise the absolute pressure in the sphere and in the release pipe, the fluid temperature at
six heights, the inner and outer wall temperatures at seven heights, the vessel mass of fluid
and solids from four load cells, and the mass flow rate through the release pipe. The load-cell
inventory, corroborated against the measured pressure and temperature, is used here as the
reference for the initial inventory and for the discharge calibration.

The pure-CO$_2$ tests are numbers 5 to 10; the sphere was filled with liquid to approximately
the middle. Three initial pressure levels are each realised as a vapour-space (gas) release and
a liquid-space (liquid) release, as summarised in Table~\ref{tab:cardice-matrix}. The gas
releases produce a large retained dry-ice bank inside the sphere, whereas the liquid releases
leave essentially no solid inside the vessel; the dry ice a liquid release produces forms
downstream of the orifice instead.

\subsection{Dense-phase experiments (SINTEF)}
\label{sec:exp-sintef}

The second campaign consists of the dense-phase depressurisation experiments performed by
SINTEF Energy Research and NTNU in the ECCSEL facility \citep{Hoydalsvik2026}. Relative to
CARDICE these experiments probe a very different part of the operating envelope, namely a
high-pressure ($\sim$120\,bar), sub-cooled dense-liquid initial state, and the fluid flashes
strongly through the discharge nozzle. The open dataset accompanies the paper.

The vessel is a vertical stainless-steel (SS316) cylinder of 273\,mm inner diameter and
1000\,mm internal height ($\sim$0.058\,m$^3$), with a 25.4\,mm wall, a 50\,mm bottom plate and
an 80\,mm lid, rated for 138\,bar and insulated with 50\,mm of Armaflex LTD. It is densely
instrumented with 26 thermocouples (fluid and wall temperatures at six heights, with four
radial positions per height: fluid on the central axis, fluid 5.5\,mm from the wall, wall 3\,mm
inside the inner surface, and the outer wall), together with four Keller pressure
transducers and three scales for the vessel weight. The vessel pressure is taken as the mean of
the transducers mounted 170\,mm from the top and 50\,mm from the bottom; two further transducers
measure the pressure immediately before and at the nozzle throat. The raw signals are sampled at
1\,kHz and averaged to 0.5\,Hz.

4.5\,mm (inlet diameter 32\,mm, half-angle $60^\circ$) in one of two configurations: directly

\section{Results and validation}
\label{sec:results}


The model is applied to all fifteen experiments described in Section~\ref{sec:experimental}
using the sub-models of Section~\ref{sec:model}. For each case the initial liquid level is
calibrated to the measured load-cell inventory, and the orifice discharge coefficient and, for
liquid releases, the non-equilibrium factor are set as described in
Section~\ref{sec:discharge}. The measured traces are time-shifted to the model: the gas
releases, which decline in pressure from the onset, are anchored at a mid-blowdown reference
pressure, whereas the slow liquid drains are anchored on the onset of the inventory decline.
Below the triple point the gas-contact wall uses the Churchill--Chu free-convection coefficient
and the wetted wall the Cooper boiling correlation (Section~\ref{sec:heat}). In the temperature
panels of the comparison figures the measured data are shown as bands over the vertical array of
thermocouples. For the CARDICE sphere two bands are used: for the internal fluid, an upper band
spanning the three uppermost thermocouples (the vapour space) and a lower band spanning the three
lowest (the liquid and solid region); for the inside wall, an upper band from the three uppermost
sensors and a lower band from the four lowest (the wetted and dry-ice region). For the SINTEF
cylinder the six fluid and six inside-wall thermocouples are each shown as a single
minimum-to-maximum band with its median.

\subsection{Low- and medium-pressure blowdown (CARDICE)}
\label{sec:results-cardice}



The central experimental contrast, a large retained dry-ice bank for the gas releases and
essentially none for the liquid releases, is reproduced across the whole set.
Table~\ref{tab:cardice-results} compares the modelled and measured initial inventory, retained
dry ice and minimum wetted-wall temperature.



For the gas releases (tests 5, 7 and 9) the model retains 266, 302 and 392\,kg of in-vessel dry
ice against measured values of 217, 298 and 412\,kg. The agreement is best for test 7, where
the retained mass is reproduced to within 2\%; test 5 is over-predicted and test 9 slightly
under-predicted. The over-prediction for test 5 is consistent with its wall sensible-heat store,
which sets the sublimation load and which the lumped wall description cannot resolve spatially.
In each case the blowdown follows the observed three-stage behaviour (an initial gas/liquid
depressurisation, a multi-hour pressure plateau at the triple point while the liquid freezes,
and a final gas/solid sublimation descent), and the wetted (bottom) wall cools to
approximately $-75\,^\circ$C, in agreement with the coldest measured sensors. The comparison for
test~7 is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cardice-t7}.

The liquid releases (tests 6, 8 and 10) drain out through the bottom connection before the vessel

without solid formation \citep{Vaillant2021, Drescher2022, Teberikler2022}. The model reproduces
this: it forms no solid and empties to a residual of cold CO$_2$ vapour that fills the vessel at
atmospheric pressure (5.0, 3.2 and 4.6\,kg for tests 6, 8 and 10), matching the measured
end-of-test inventory (4.2, 3.8 and 5.1\,kg). The pressure and inventory histories are captured
well; Figure~\ref{fig:cardice-t8} shows test~8, for which the modelled liquid drain and the time
to empty match the data.

The principal discrepancy is the bulk gas temperature for the gas releases, which the
zero-dimensional model under-predicts relative to the warm upper region of the vessel. The real
vapour is strongly stratified, warm near the top and cold near the interface, which a
single well-mixed gas node cannot represent, as it returns an average and mixes in cold
boil-off vapour. This is a fundamental limitation of the lumped description rather than a
heat-transfer deficiency, and the same behaviour is reported for the zero-dimensional tool
Vessfire \citep{Vaillant2021}.

\subsection{Dense-phase blowdown (SINTEF)}
\label{sec:results-dense}

The dense-phase experiments start as a sub-cooled liquid at approximately 120\,bar and flash
into the two-phase region during the initial, rapid depressurisation. As for CARDICE, the
no-riser (gas) and riser (liquid) configurations behave very differently, and both are
reproduced.

For the no-riser (gas) releases the dense liquid remains in the vessel, cools to the triple
point and freezes. The model retains approximately 8.4, 7.8 and 8.1\,kg of dry ice for Exp71,
Exp72 and Exp75, in close agreement with the measured value of approximately 8.4\,kg for Exp71.
The comparison for Exp72 is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:munke-exp72}.

\label{fig:munke-exp72}
\end{figure*}

For the riser (liquid) releases the vessel drains through the riser and empties before the
triple point, leaving no in-vessel solid, as observed. The rapid dense-phase depressurisation
is captured well: the pressure falls from approximately 120\,bar, flashes onto the two-phase
saturation line at approximately 52\,bar, and follows the measured decline closely, while the
modelled gas and liquid/solid temperatures bracket the measured stratification band. The
comparison for Exp53 is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:munke-exp53}.

\begin{figure*}[!tbh]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/munke_exp53}
\caption{SINTEF Exp53 (riser liquid release, 119.5\,bar, 8.0\,mm nozzle): model (dashed) versus
measured data. Panels as in Figure~\ref{fig:cardice-t7}. The vessel drains through the riser and
empties before the triple point; the first 100\,s, covering the active blowdown, are shown.}
\label{fig:munke-exp53}
\end{figure*}

Two limitations are noted for the dense-phase cases. As for CARDICE, the bulk gas temperature
reflects the well-mixed assumption and does not resolve the vertical stratification observed in
the fluid thermocouples. In addition, in the gas/solid tail of the no-riser cases the modelled
dry-ice-contact wall tracks the sublimation temperature and reads colder than the measurements;
the relatively thick wall and the thin ice layer in these experiments allow lateral conduction
in the wall to re-warm the ice-contact metal, an effect a one-dimensional wall treatment does
not capture.

\section{Sensitivity studies}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

To assess the numerical robustness of the model and the influence of its principal modelling
choices, four sensitivity studies are presented: the time-step of the explicit integration
(Section~\ref{sec:sens-dt}), the plateau gas-liquid interphase coefficient
(Section~\ref{sec:sens-htc}), the residual-heel dependence of the solid formed during a liquid
release (Section~\ref{sec:sens-heel}), and the vessel-wall thermal mass
(Section~\ref{sec:sens-wall}). A representative case is used for each: for the two CARDICE-based
studies the gas release test~7 (which develops the full plateau and descent) and the liquid
release test~8; for the two SINTEF-based studies the gas release Exp72.

\subsection{Time-step convergence}
\label{sec:sens-dt}

The mass and energy balances are integrated with an explicit (forward Euler) scheme, so the time
step must be small enough for the result to be converged. Table~\ref{tab:sens-dt} lists the
retained dry ice and the temperature minima for CARDICE test~7 as the step is reduced from 40 to
2\,s about the base value of 20\,s. The retained dry ice changes by about 0.4\% between the base
step and the finest, and the wall and gas temperature minima move by less than 0.1\,$^\circ$C. The
dense-phase SINTEF cases are integrated at a base step of 0.1\,s; for Exp72 the retained dry ice
is 7.82, 7.84, 7.86 and 7.87\,kg at 0.2, 0.1, 0.05 and 0.02\,s, a spread of 0.7\% across a tenfold
reduction. The base steps are therefore adequate and are used throughout.

\subsection{Plateau gas-liquid interphase coefficient}
\label{sec:sens-htc}

During the triple-point plateau of a gas release the warm vapour is only weakly coupled to the
cold liquid and solid, and the plateau interphase coefficient $h_{gl}$ (the below-triple
gas-liquid coupling, distinct from the two-sided coefficient used above the triple point,
Section~\ref{sec:heat}) is kept near zero. The physical basis is that the dry ice forms a porous, foam-like solid
that floats on and covers the liquid surface \citep{Jamois2023}, insulating the vapour from the
condensate. Figure~\ref{fig:sens-htc} shows the effect of raising $h_{gl}$ from zero on the
plateau vapour temperature. Increasing the coefficient steadily erodes the measured vapour
superheat: for CARDICE test~7 the superheat above the triple point falls from about 21\,$^\circ$C
at $h_{gl}=0$ to about 12\,$^\circ$C at 10\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$, moving the vapour out of the
measured band, while the retained dry ice changes only modestly (302 to 293\,kg). For reference,
a two-sided natural-convection estimate for the same interface predicts a coefficient of order
10 to 20\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$ (the shaded range in Figure~\ref{fig:sens-htc}), which would cool the
vapour well below the observations. The measured superheat is reproduced only when the effective
plateau coefficient is far smaller than the natural-convection value, consistent with the
insulating dry-ice layer. The same comparison for the dense-phase SINTEF gas release Exp72
(Figure~\ref{fig:sens-htc-munke}) is less discriminating: the modelled vapour remains within the
measured band for all coefficients tested, because the triple-point plateau is brief, so the
near-zero value used for the CARDICE cases is compatible with the SINTEF data although not strongly
constrained by them.

\begin{figure}[!tbh]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/sens_htc}
\caption{Plateau gas-liquid interphase coefficient sensitivity for the CARDICE test~7 gas release.
The modelled vapour temperature at the mid-plateau (3.5\,h) is shown against the imposed plateau
coefficient $h_{gl}$; the horizontal band and line are the measured vapour band and average, and
the shaded vertical band is the range the two-sided natural-convection model would give for the
same interface. The measured superheat is matched only for a plateau coefficient far below the
natural-convection value, consistent with an insulating floating dry-ice layer.}
\label{fig:sens-htc}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[!tbh]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/sens_htc_munke}
\caption{As Figure~\ref{fig:sens-htc}, for the SINTEF Exp72 gas release (vapour temperature at
200\,s during the plateau). The modelled vapour remains within the measured band for all
coefficients tested, so the plateau coefficient is weakly constrained here (the dense-phase plateau
is brief); the near-zero value is compatible with the data.}
\label{fig:sens-htc-munke}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Solid formed during a liquid release and the residual heel}
\label{sec:sens-heel}

A liquid-space release draws liquid from the bottom connection and empties the vessel of liquid
before the triple point is reached, so no dry ice is retained inside the vessel, in agreement with
the reported experiments and their simulations \citep{Vaillant2021, Drescher2022, Teberikler2022}.
In-vessel solid could nonetheless form if a residual liquid heel were left when the pressure
crossed the triple point, for example if the draw point stood above the vessel floor.
Figure~\ref{fig:sens-heel} demonstrates this for CARDICE test~8 by artificially raising the draw
level: the retained in-vessel dry ice grows from zero for the bottom-pole drain to about 1.7, 5.0
and 14.2\,kg for heels of 60, 80 and 120\,mm. For the actual bottom-pole connection the heel is
negligible and no solid forms, as observed.

\begin{figure}[!tbh]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/sens_heel}
\caption{CARDICE test~8 (liquid release): retained in-vessel dry ice as a function of an imposed
residual liquid heel (draw level above the floor). The bottom-pole drain of the experiment leaves
no heel and forms no in-vessel solid; a raised draw would freeze the residual liquid.}
\label{fig:sens-heel}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Vessel-wall thermal mass}
\label{sec:sens-wall}

The SINTEF cylinder has a non-uniform wall, a 25.4\,mm cylindrical shell closed by a 50\,mm bottom
plate and an 80\,mm lid, which is represented in the model by a uniform wall of the shell
thickness. Figure~\ref{fig:sens-wall} shows the modelled vapour temperature for Exp72 as the
uniform wall thickness is increased from the 25.4\,mm shell, together with the measured vapour band
and average. The tail is strongly sensitive to the wall thermal mass: increasing the thickness from
25.4 to 50\,mm (raising the wall mass from about 220 to 490\,kg) warms the tail vapour by several
tens of degrees while reducing the retained dry ice from 7.8 to 5.1\,kg. The measured vapour
re-warms markedly in the tail as the upper wall returns heat to the residual gas, and the two
targets pull in opposite directions. The shell-thickness model reproduces the retained dry ice
(7.8\,kg, close to the measured value) but re-warms the tail vapour too weakly, running below the
measured band; increasing the thickness warms the tail vapour into the measured range but
progressively under-predicts the retained solid. No single uniform thickness reproduces both the
retained solid and the tail re-warming: the retained solid is set by the near-surface material that
stays cold against the ice, whereas the re-warming requires heat from the warmer upper wall. This
tension is a direct consequence of representing the wall by a single uniform thickness, and it
motivates a two-dimensional (axisymmetric) conjugate wall description, in which axial conduction
lets the ice-contact region stay cold while the upper wall re-warms the vapour; this is left for
future work.

\begin{figure}[!tbh]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/sens_wall_thickness}
\caption{SINTEF Exp72 (gas release): modelled vapour temperature for uniform wall thicknesses of
25.4, 50, 80 and 120\,mm (wall masses of about 220, 490, 890 and 1570\,kg), with the measured
vapour band and average from the upper thermocouples. The 25.4\,mm shell reproduces the retained
dry ice but re-warms the tail vapour too weakly, running below the measured band; heavier walls
re-warm the vapour but under-predict the retained solid, so no single uniform thickness reproduces
both.}
\label{fig:sens-wall}
\end{figure}
